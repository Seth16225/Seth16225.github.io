Metal Detector

A beat-frequency metal detector built from discrete MOSFETs on a custom Altium PCB — two LC oscillators, a MOSFET mixer, a low-pass filter, and a source follower that drives the speaker. Sweep the coil over metal and the pitch drops.

ESE 3190 · UPenn Spring 2026 Altium LTspice Analog Design

How it works

Beat-frequency detection

Two LC oscillators run just a kilohertz apart: one fixed at ~50 kHz, one tuned by the hand-wound search coil at ~49 kHz. A mixer sums them, and because a MOSFET's drain current follows a square law, mixing produces a difference tone — about 1 kHz, right in the middle of the audible band. A low-pass filter throws away the 50 kHz carriers and keeps that beat note.

Bringing the coil near metal induces eddy currents in it, which lower the coil's inductance and raise the variable oscillator's frequency. The gap between the two oscillators shrinks, so the beat tone drops in pitch — the whole detector is that one effect, made audible.

Oscillators

The search coil was wound by hand to 5.36 mH — large enough to project a usable magnetic field (small coils induce weak eddy currents and make an insensitive detector), small enough that the resonating capacitor stays compact on the PCB. Solving f = 1/(2π√LC) for 49 kHz gave 1.96 nF; the fixed oscillator pairs a 10 mH inductor with 1.01 nF for 50 kHz.

Mixer

A differential pair sums both oscillators. The square-law drain current generates the sum-and-difference products, and the first common-source stage amplifies the result into a 2 kΩ load.

Filter

A 10 kΩ / 2.2 nF RC filter cuts at 7.23 kHz — far below the 50 kHz carriers, far above the beat note. The 10 kΩ value was picked to sit well above the preceding stage's 2 kΩ load so it wouldn't drag the gain down.

Output

A second CS amp (2.2 kΩ load) adds loudness, then a source follower buffers into the speaker. Driving an 8 Ω load straight off a CS amp fails — its output resistance is high, so almost all the signal drops across the Thévenin resistance instead of the speaker. The follower's low output impedance fixes the transfer.

Measured results

Following the signal down the chain

Every stage was probed at its test point on a powered board. You can watch the 50 kHz carrier survive the mixer and first amplifier, then disappear at the filter and leave only the audible beat note behind.

Theory vs. bench

Small-signal gain predictions held up only where the signal stayed small. The mixer and CS stages swing hard enough to clip against the rails and fall out of saturation, so their measured gains land well below the hand calculations — while the source follower, which barely swings the device, matched almost exactly.

Outcome

It found everything on demo day

Sweeping the coil over each target dropped the pitch exactly as the theory predicts: eddy currents lower the coil's inductance, the variable oscillator climbs toward the fixed one, and the beat note falls. Every object was detected.

The honest gap between simulation and hardware was the filter. LTspice produced a clean sine at the output; the real board still carries visible 50 kHz ripple, because the carriers are the largest-amplitude signals in the chain and a single-pole RC only attenuates so much. Raising the filter capacitance would push the cutoff lower and clean it up further — the next thing I'd change. The oscillators also landed at 51.1 and 49.1 kHz rather than 50 and 49, giving a ~2 kHz beat instead of 1 kHz, which traces back to parasitic L and C plus component tolerance.

What I took away

Small-signal models tell you where to start, not what you'll measure. The stages that swing hardest deviated most from hand calculations, and the fix each time was on the bench — trimming bias points with the potentiometers and probing stage by stage through the test points I'd placed during layout.
